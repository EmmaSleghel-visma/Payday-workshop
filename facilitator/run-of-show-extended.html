Four people, 08:30, and they don't know you

“Shout it out” is a thirty-person move. With two to five people who have never met you, at half past eight in the morning, an open question to the room lands in silence — and then you feel it, and you start filling gaps, and you talk for six hours. All twelve questions in this playbook carry a written escape, so a dead question costs you fifteen seconds instead of your confidence. Here is the ladder they use.

1
Ask, then count to five. Silence is thinking. Most facilitators break it at two seconds and answer their own question. Count five in your head, looking at nobody in particular.
2
Downgrade to binary. “Hands up if you'd say yes.” Physical, unambiguous, and it works with two people. A hand is a much smaller commitment than a sentence.
3
Thirty seconds with the person next to you. Then the pair reports, not the individual — half the exposure. If it is a two-person room this is awkward; skip to rung 4.
4
Answer it wrong yourself. “I'll guess you'd say X — tell me I'm wrong.” The strongest move on the ladder. People correct far more readily than they volunteer, and correcting you costs them nothing socially.
5
Answer it and move on. The question already did its work by making them think it. Never let a dead question cost more than fifteen seconds.
The standing rule Never ask two open questions in a row that got no answer. If two die, switch to writing for the next ten minutes — “put it in the channel” — and come back to speaking after the next hands-on, when they have something concrete to report. And remember which of them did speak: with four people you can hand the next question to that person by name and it reads as inclusion, not interrogation.
The questions that always get an answer Factual ones only they can answer. “When does that money arrive?” to an Icelandic speaker looking at Pay date 9/4/2026 gets a reply every single time, because they know something you don't and the answer costs them no opinion — and if two of them read it differently, better still. Use one of those early — 12:00 their time, the app tour — to prove the room is allowed to speak. Opinion questions land much better after that.

SayTokens and context30–37

Five minutes on tokens and context, because two concepts explain nearly every weird agent behaviour you'll see today.

Tokens: roughly four characters each, ten to fifteen per line of code. The context window is input plus output. Why you care: a large codebase does not fit, and everything the agent reads competes for space with everything it has to remember.

And context is the entire conversation — every message, every file it opened, every command output — resent on every single call. Nothing is “remembered” between turns except what's in that window. Internalise that and agent behaviour stops being mysterious. It didn't forget. It ran out of room, or something else crowded it out.

SayManaging the window, and the standing challenges38, 39

So when do you start fresh? When your opening prompt was vague. When the history is messy. When you're seeing the same error repeatedly. And the big tell — when old, rejected solutions start reappearing. That's context rot: the model is following something you thought you'd moved past.

New chat equals fresh context. It's cheap. Use it liberally. With agents this is doubly true — a long polluted session produces worse code than a short focused one.

And the standing challenges on this slide aren't solved, they're managed: losing context, non-determinism, no knowledge of your private code, hallucinations, a knowledge cut-off. Every technique today is a way of managing one of them. Never accept output uncritically.
